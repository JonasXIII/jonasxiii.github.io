How This AI Thinks

A plain-language tour of every toggle in the playground, with things to try so you can watch each one work.

The basic idea: looking ahead

An Abalone AI can't "see" a good move the way you recognize a face. It has to search. It plays out an imaginary sequence of moves: "if I move here, my opponent will probably respond there, then I'd respond here…" and so on, a few moves deep. That's minimax search. Both sides are assumed to play their best move, and the AI picks whichever first move leads to the best outcome once all that future play is accounted for.

The lookahead depth slider controls how many moves ("plies") ahead it searches. Depth 1 just asks "what's the best immediate move?" Depth 4 asks "what's the best move, assuming my opponent then plays their best reply, and I play my best reply to that, three more times?" Deeper search sees further into the consequences of a move. It also gets slow fast, because the number of possible sequences explodes exponentially with depth.

Why an evaluation function is needed at all

A full game of Abalone can run for dozens of moves. No computer can search all the way to the end of the game in reasonable time. The tree of possibilities is astronomically large. So the search has to stop a few moves ahead and guess how good the resulting position is. That guess is the evaluation function: a formula that looks at a board and returns a number. Positive favors the AI, negative favors the opponent.

In this playground, the evaluation is a weighted sum of six independent ideas ("heuristics") you can turn on/off and reweight in AI Settings:

  • Marble differential. How many marbles each side has left. The most direct signal, since losing 6 marbles is literally how you lose.
  • Center control. Marbles near the middle of the board are harder to push off, since they have more neighbors backing them up, and they threaten more directions at once.
  • Cohesion. Marbles that stay grouped with same-color neighbors are safer. Isolated ones are easy 2-on-1 or 3-on-1 targets.
  • Edge safety. Marbles sitting on the outer ring are one push away from falling off the board entirely.
  • Triple formations. A line of 3 same-color marbles is the strongest attacking shape there is. It can push 1 or 2 opposing marbles.
  • Mobility. How many legal moves a side has. A very accurate signal of flexibility, and the most expensive one to compute. Toggling it off noticeably speeds up deep searches, which is why even the strongest built-in bot leaves it off.

Try this: turn every heuristic off except marble differential, then watch your AI play. It only cares about immediate captures. Positioning stops existing.

Alpha-beta pruning: same answer, far less work

Searching every possible sequence of moves ("plain minimax") gets slow fast. Alpha-beta pruning skips over branches of the search tree that can't possibly change the outcome. If the AI already knows a rival move guarantees a better result, it stops examining any line once it finds one reply bad enough to prove that line is worse. Pruning never changes which move the AI picks. It just gets there faster.

A real measurement from this project: searching the opening position to depth 2 with default settings examined 1,981 positions with pruning off and 400 positions with pruning on, a 5x reduction, landing on the same best move with the same score.

Try this: turn Alpha-beta pruning off in AI Settings. The lookahead slider gets capped at 2, because without pruning depth 3 takes too long to stay responsive.

Caching: never solve the same position twice

Different move sequences can arrive at the exact same board position (e.g. move A then B can land on the same board as move B then A). Re-searching a position the AI has already fully analyzed is wasted work. A transposition table remembers "I already searched this exact position to this depth, and here's what I found." If the position comes up again, the AI reuses that answer.

The "Cache hits" and "Cache size" numbers in the Thinking panel show this happening live. In Batch Run mode, later games in a batch often think noticeably faster than the first. The cache built up during earlier games is still warm.

When a new result would replace an old cached one, the cache keeps whichever came from a deeper search. A shallow re-visit never evicts a deep one, since the deep result took far more work to produce.

Cutting branches

There are two ways to make a search faster: make each position cheaper to evaluate, or look at fewer positions in the first place. The second is almost always the bigger win, because the search tree grows exponentially with depth. Cutting the number of branches explored at each level compounds. Speeding up one evaluation only ever saves a constant amount of time per position. When alpha-beta pruning and caching are both on, three extra techniques go after the branches:

  • Iterative deepening. The AI searches depth 1, then 2, then 3, and so on up to the target depth. That sounds like wasted work, since the shallow searches get redone. But each shallow pass tells the AI roughly which move is best before the next pass starts, and searching the actually-best move first is exactly what lets alpha-beta prune the most.
  • Killer moves. If a particular move caused a cutoff somewhere in the tree, proving a branch not worth exploring further, that same move gets tried early the next time the AI is choosing at that depth elsewhere. A strong push or a good defensive shift tends to recur in unrelated branches.
  • Principal variation search. Once the first move at a position is searched properly, every move after it gets a cheap "prove you're not better than what I already have" check with an artificially narrow window. Only on the rare occasion a move passes that check does it get the full, expensive re-search.
Real numbers from this project: searching the opening position to depth 4 with default settings visited 88,283 positions with plain alpha-beta ordering. With iterative deepening, killer moves, and principal variation search added on top, the same search visited only 9,277 positions, about 90% fewer, with the same final score and the same move.

None of this changes the move the AI ends up making. Only alpha-beta pruning can safely skip branches without changing the answer; these three just help it find the cutoffs faster. They are active only when alpha-beta and caching are both on. Turn either off and the AI falls back to a plain search, which is there so you always have a clean baseline to compare against.

Opening principles

The first couple of moves are well understood in Abalone: pull your back-row marbles toward the center, stay off the edges. When "Use opening principles for first moves" is on, the AI skips the full search for its first two moves per side and picks whichever legal move most improves center control and edge safety. It's faster than searching, and it follows real opening theory rather than a memorized sequence.

Why the weaker bots feel less "robotic"

A bot that always plays the mathematically-best move at a shallow depth can feel oddly predictable and easy to exploit once you spot the pattern. The two weakest bots (Novice and Apprentice) have a chance of ignoring their own search result and playing a random legal move. That makes them harder to read and more forgiving to play against. It also means they blunder sometimes. The stronger bots set the chance to zero and lean on search.

Try it yourself: Batch Run mode

Watching one game tells you what happened once. Batch Run mode plays many games back-to-back with no animation and reports the aggregate result, which is a much better way to answer questions like:

  • Does turning alpha-beta off actually make the AI weaker, or just slower? (Configure two identical AIs except for that one toggle, run 10-20 games, compare win rates.)
  • How much does lookahead depth alone matter? Pit a depth-1 AI against a depth-4 AI with the same heuristics.
  • Is the mobility heuristic worth its cost? Compare think-time and win rate with it on vs off.
  • How strong is each built-in bot, really? Run your default-configured AI against each level and see where the win rate crosses 50%.

Every batch result is saved locally so you can build up a small experiment log over time.

Automating the search: "Find best settings"

Manually running batches for every combination of settings doesn't scale. Lookahead alone has 4 values, and with 6 heuristics that can each be on or off, the full space runs to thousands of combinations. Testing all of them exhaustively (a "grid search") would take far too many games to be practical.

Instead, the "🔬 Find best settings" option in Batch Run mode uses a much cheaper strategy called hill climbing. Start from a baseline configuration. Randomly change one or two things about it (nudge the lookahead, flip a toggle, reweight a heuristic) to produce a handful of candidates, play each one against your chosen bot for a few games, and keep whichever won the most, baseline included. The next round mutates that winner, so the search stays biased toward whatever has already proven itself. Repeat for several rounds and the configuration climbs toward stronger settings.

This is a simplified, single-solution version of an evolutionary search (specifically "(1+λ) elitist hill climbing" if you want the formal name). It's a common, cheap way to tune settings when the space is too big to search exhaustively and each individual test costs you a batch of games.

It won't necessarily find the best configuration possible. Like any hill-climbing search it can settle on a "local peak" that a very different starting point would have beaten, and win-rate estimates from a handful of games are noisy, especially against a bot with randomness of its own. Treat the result as a good place to keep experimenting from.

Every tested configuration is kept, not just the winner, so the results page can also show a "what mattered" breakdown: the average win rate across every config tried, split by whether each setting was on or off. A big gap between a setting's "on" and "off" averages means it's driving the outcome against that opponent. A small gap means it probably isn't.

Try this: run "Find best settings" against a couple of different bot levels from the same baseline, then compare the winning configurations. What beats Novice's shallow, half-random play is often not what beats Master.
